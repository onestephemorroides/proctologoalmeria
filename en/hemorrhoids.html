Quick answer: haemorrhoids (piles) are vascular cushions in the anal canal that bleed or prolapse when they become inflamed. Most can be treated without surgery, in the clinic: rubber band ligation (grades II and III) or foam sclerotherapy (grades I and II). Surgery is reserved for grade IV or when outpatient treatment is not enough. Any rectal bleeding should be assessed by a doctor to confirm the cause.

What are haemorrhoids, symptoms and warning signs

What are they? Haemorrhoids are normal vascular structures of the anal canal that help with faecal continence. We speak of haemorrhoidal disease when they become inflamed, bleed or prolapse and cause symptoms. Symptomatic haemorrhoids are very common: many adults are affected at some point. They are more common with chronic constipation, a sedentary lifestyle, pregnancy or a low-fibre diet.

Most common symptoms

  • Rectal bleeding, usually bright red, when passing a stool or on the toilet paper
  • Prolapse: a lump or tissue coming out of the anus
  • Persistent anal itching or irritation
  • Pain or discomfort when passing a stool or sitting
  • Staining of underwear
  • Feeling of incomplete emptying

Not all bleeding is from haemorrhoids. A fissure, a polyp or, less often, a colorectal tumour can cause the same symptoms. See a doctor without delay if there is:

  • Dark blood, or blood mixed with the stool
  • A recent change in bowel habit
  • Weight loss, tiredness or anaemia
  • A family history of colorectal cancer
  • Persistent bleeding from the age of 50

A hard, painful, purple lump next to the anus may be a thrombosed haemorrhoid: it needs urgent assessment. More information: rectal bleeding: common causes and haemorrhoid symptoms.

Haemorrhoid grades (Goligher scale) and treatment

The grade determines which treatment fits best. The final decision is made after the examination, because two patients with the same grade may need different things.

GradeWhat happensUsual treatment
IBleeding without prolapse; the haemorrhoids cannot be seen from outsideDietary measures and foam sclerotherapy; ligation if bleeding persists
IIProlapse on passing a stool and go back in by themselvesRubber band ligation or foam sclerotherapy
IIIProlapse and have to be pushed back in with a fingerRubber band ligation, in one or several sessions
IVPermanent prolapse, cannot be pushed backUsually surgery

Treatments: ligation, sclerotherapy and surgery

In the clinic Dr. Jaime Jorge Cerrudo carries out the examination, the diagnosis and, when indicated, the treatment in the same visit.

Rubber band ligation — See step by step →
A rubber band is placed at the base of the haemorrhoid, which stops receiving blood and drops off within 5 to 10 days. It is the treatment of choice for grades II and III. It is done in the clinic, without anaesthesia, and may leave a feeling of fullness or pressure for 24 to 48 hours. Sometimes more than one session is needed.
Foam sclerotherapy — See sclerotherapy →
A sclerosing solution is injected as a microfoam that closes the haemorrhoidal vessel. It is especially useful for grades I and II when bleeding is the main symptom, and recovery is usually immediate.

Surgery. Haemorrhoidectomy and other surgical techniques are reserved for grade IV, for cases that do not respond to the above and for some acute thrombosed haemorrhoids. It requires anaesthesia and a longer recovery. In general, most patients do not need it. When is surgery necessary?

After treatment, emergencies and prevention

What is normal after ligation or sclerotherapy

  • Some blood mixed with the stool during the first 5 to 14 days
  • A feeling of weight or pressure in the anus for 24 to 48 hours
  • Mild discomfort that is usually controlled with an ordinary painkiller

You can take anti-inflammatory drugs as normal. If you take blood thinners, speak to the clinic before treatment and do not stop them on your own. Contact the clinic immediately if there is heavy bleeding with clots, fever, severe pain that is getting worse, or you cannot pass urine.

Thrombosed haemorrhoid. This is a clot inside an external haemorrhoid: a hard, painful, purple lump appears. If the thrombosis is external, the haemorrhoid can be excised with a procedure during the first 72 hours, which improves symptoms and stops that same haemorrhoid from becoming inflamed again. After that time, excision gives no advantage for how the episode evolves, and it is usually managed with pain relief and local measures. If the episode is internal, treatment is always medical and excision is not recommended. Removing only the clot (thrombectomy) is not recommended. Do not wait for it to go away on its own.

How to prevent haemorrhoids and stop them coming back

  • Fibre: about 25 to 30 grams a day (fruit, vegetables, pulses, wholegrain cereals)
  • Drink water regularly throughout the day
  • Do not spend more than 5 minutes on the toilet or strain; leave your phone outside
  • Do not ignore the urge to go to the toilet
  • Daily physical activity and avoid long hours of sitting
  • Treat chronic constipation or diarrhoea rather than living with them

Frequently asked questions about haemorrhoids

Does rubber band ligation hurt?
The band is placed in the anal canal, above the dentate line, an area with very little pain sensitivity. For this reason most patients feel no pain during the ligation. There may be a mild sensation of pressure or fullness during the first 24–48 hours afterwards.
How long does rubber band ligation take to work?
The ligated haemorrhoid loses its blood supply and falls off within 5–10 days. Bleeding usually stops within the first week. Improvement in prolapse may take 2–4 weeks. In some cases a second session is needed.
Can I return to work the same day?
It depends on the procedure. After foam sclerotherapy, recovery is immediate and you can resume normal activity the same day. After rubber band ligation, most patients return to their usual routine within 24–48 hours. Neither procedure requires hospital admission or general anaesthesia.
Can haemorrhoids come back after treatment?
Non-surgical haemorrhoid procedures have a high success rate after 1 or 2 sessions. The risk of recurrence can be minimised with lifestyle changes: a high-fibre diet, adequate hydration and avoiding constipation. Should they recur, the procedure can be repeated without difficulty.
When is surgery actually necessary for haemorrhoids?
Surgery (haemorrhoidectomy) is indicated for grade IV haemorrhoids with permanent prolapse, when outpatient procedures have failed, or in cases of acute thrombosed haemorrhoids. In general, most haemorrhoids do not require surgery.
Which doctor treats haemorrhoids?
A proctologist, also called a colorectal surgeon. In this clinic, Dr. Jaime Jorge Cerrudo, a surgeon specialising in coloproctology, makes the diagnosis and carries out the treatment.
Do I need a colonoscopy before haemorrhoids are treated?
Not always. It is recommended when there are warning signs, a recent change in bowel habit, a family history of colorectal cancer, or from the age at which screening begins, to rule out other causes of bleeding.
Can haemorrhoids be treated if I take blood thinners?
It depends on the drug and the reason you take it. After ligation the risk of delayed bleeding is higher, so each case is assessed individually. Never stop a blood thinner on your own.
What about pregnancy?
Haemorrhoids are very common during pregnancy and after birth, and often improve after delivery with dietary measures and local treatment. Procedures are reserved for specific cases once the timing has been considered.
Do creams and ointments work?
They relieve itching and discomfort temporarily, but they do not remove the haemorrhoid. If symptoms last more than a week or two, or there is bleeding, a medical assessment is advisable.
